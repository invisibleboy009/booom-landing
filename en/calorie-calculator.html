BMR vs TDEE — what's what

BMR (basal metabolic rate) is the energy your body burns at complete rest — just to keep you functioning. TDEE (total daily energy expenditure) is your total daily burn including movement and training. TDEE is your maintenance intake — how much to eat to keep your weight the same.

For fat loss, eat slightly below TDEE (~20% deficit); for muscle gain, slightly above (~10% surplus). Extreme deficits don't pay off — you lose muscle and energy.

How the calculator works out your intake

It uses the Mifflin-St Jeor formula, which studies repeatedly show to be one of the most accurate for the general population:

  • Men: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age + 5
  • Women: BMR = 10 × weight (kg) + 6.25 × height (cm) − 5 × age − 161

BMR is then multiplied by an activity factor and you get your TDEE:

ActivityFactorWhat it looks like
Sedentary1.2Office job, no exercise
Light1.3751–3 workouts per week
Moderate1.553–5 workouts per week
High1.7256–7 workouts per week
Very high1.9Two workouts a day or a physically demanding job

If you're torn between two levels, pick the lower one — people most often overestimate their activity.

Example calculation

Man, 30 years old, 80 kg, 180 cm, moderate activity

BMR = 10 × 80 + 6.25 × 180 − 5 × 30 + 5 = 1,780 kcal. TDEE = 1,780 × 1.55 = 2,759 kcal.

Fat loss: 2,207 kcal (160 g protein, 254 g carbs, 61 g fat). Muscle gain: 3,035 kcal.

Woman, 30 years old, 65 kg, 168 cm, moderate activity

BMR = 10 × 65 + 6.25 × 168 − 5 × 30 − 161 = 1,389 kcal. TDEE = 1,389 × 1.55 = 2,153 kcal.

Fat loss: 1,722 kcal (130 g protein, 193 g carbs, 48 g fat). Muscle gain: 2,368 kcal.

Macros made simple

  • Protein: ~2 g per kg of body weight (preserves muscle, keeps you full) — 4 kcal per gram
  • Fat: ~25% of calories (hormones, vitamin absorption) — 9 kcal per gram
  • Carbs: the rest of your calories (fuel for training) — 4 kcal per gram

Keep fat at around 20% of calories or more in the long run, otherwise hormones and vitamin absorption suffer. Within reason, you can shift carbs up or down depending on what suits you and how you feel when training.

How to set a deficit or surplus

  • Fat loss: 15 to 20% below TDEE. Realistic and sustainable is roughly 0.5 to 1% of body weight per week.
  • Muscle gain: 5 to 10% above TDEE. A bigger surplus mostly adds fat, not muscle.
  • Don't stay below your BMR long term without professional supervision.
  • After 2 to 3 weeks compare your average weight. If it isn't moving, adjust your intake by 100 to 200 kcal.

Most common mistakes

  • Not logging the small stuff. Oil, sauces, drinks and “tasting” while cooking can add up to several hundred calories.
  • Strict week, free weekend. A weekend surplus can wipe out the whole week's deficit.
  • Overestimating activity in the calculator. Your workout lasts an hour, you sit for the other 23. Pick the level that matches your whole week.
  • Weighing yourself once every few days. Your weight fluctuates by 1 to 2 kg with water and food. Track the weekly average.

Where the calculator isn't enough

The calculator is designed for healthy adults. With a thyroid condition your metabolism can be different — see the guide thyroid and nutrition (Slovak). Likewise, talk to a doctor if you're pregnant, breastfeeding, or have diabetes or an eating disorder. If you also want to know your body composition, check the body fat calculator.

Frequently asked questions

How is daily calorie intake calculated?
BMR (Mifflin-St Jeor) × activity factor = TDEE. For fat loss about 20% is subtracted, for muscle gain about 10% is added.
What's the difference between BMR and TDEE?
BMR is the energy your body burns at complete rest. TDEE is your total daily expenditure including movement and training — in other words, the calories at which you maintain your weight.
How much protein should I eat?
A proven range is 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of body weight, especially when cutting and strength training. The calculator uses 2 g per kg. More in the article How much protein per day.
How big a deficit is safe?
Usually 15 to 20% below TDEE is recommended, which corresponds to losing roughly 0.5 to 1% of body weight per week. Harsher deficits raise the risk of muscle loss, fatigue and nutrient deficiencies.
Is the calculator accurate?
It's an estimate — real expenditure differs between people by several hundred calories. Track your average weight over 2 to 3 weeks and adjust your intake by 100 to 200 kcal if nothing is happening.
Do I have to eat exactly that many calories every day?
No. The weekly average matters more. A day with slightly higher intake and a day with lower intake even out if you stick to the plan most of the week.
Who is the calculator not suitable for?
If you're under 18, pregnant or breastfeeding, or have a medical condition (diabetes, thyroid disease, eating disorders), talk to a doctor or a nutrition professional.
