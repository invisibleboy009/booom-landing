What 1RM is and what it's good for

1RM (one rep max) is the heaviest weight you can lift for one clean rep. It's the basic reference in strength training — training percentages are built on it, telling you what weight to use for strength, hypertrophy or endurance.

Testing a true 1RM is demanding and risky, so it's most often estimated from a set of several reps. This calculator uses the Epley formula (1RM = weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30)) and, for comparison, Brzycki as well (1RM = weight × 36 ÷ (37 − reps)).

How to enter the right data

The calculator is only as accurate as the numbers you give it. Here's the routine that gives the most reliable estimate:

  1. Pick a basic compound lift — squat, deadlift, bench press, overhead press or row. For machines and isolation exercises the estimate is less reliable.
  2. Warm up properly — build the weight up gradually and keep the first sets light.
  3. Do a set of 3 to 8 reps with technique that would hold up even on the first rep. Leave at least one rep in reserve, or have a spotter.
  4. Enter the weight and reps into the calculator.
  5. Measure the same way when comparing over time — same exercise, same range of motion, similar fatigue.

How the formulas differ — an example

If you lift 100 kg for 5 reps, the individual formulas give:

Formula1RM estimate
Epley (main calculation in the calculator)116.7 kg
Brzycki112.5 kg
Lombardi117.5 kg
O'Conner112.5 kg

The table shows the exact formula values; the calculator rounds its result to the nearest 0.5 kg (116.7 is displayed as 116.5 kg). A spread of roughly 112 to 118 kg is normal. Epley and Lombardi come out higher, Brzycki and O'Conner more conservative. If in doubt, plan your training around the lower number — it's safer to start lighter and add weight.

Example: your squat 1RM is 120 kg and you want to build muscle. 75% of 1RM is 90 kg — you do 3 to 4 sets of 8 to 10 reps. To train strength, pick 85% (102.5 kg) for 4 to 5 reps.

You get the most accurate estimate from a set of 2 to 10 reps. At very high reps the estimate runs too high, because endurance, not maximal strength, drives the performance.

Most common mistakes

  • Too many reps. A set of 15 to 20 reps says more about endurance than strength, and the estimate comes out inflated.
  • Testing on a bad day. Fatigue, too little sleep or hunger drag your performance down. One number is not a trend — track progress over several weeks.
  • Comparing different exercises or depth. A half squat and a full-depth squat are two different exercises.
  • Treating 1RM as a fixed number. It changes. Recalculate it every 4 to 6 weeks.
  • A true max test without a spotter. Never test a true 1RM alone without a spotter and a warm-up.

Frequently asked questions

What is 1RM (one rep max)?
1RM (one rep max) is the heaviest weight you can lift for one clean rep in a given exercise. It's used to plan training percentages and track strength.
How is the estimated 1RM calculated?
From the weight and number of reps using proven formulas. The best known is Epley: 1RM = weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30). Accuracy is highest at 2 to 10 reps — the more reps, the bigger the error.
How many reps should I enter?
Ideally a set of 3 to 8 reps with clean technique and at least one rep left in the tank. If you did just one rep, you already know your 1RM; above 10 reps the estimate runs too high.
Which training percentages should I use?
In short: strength 85–100% (1–5 reps), hypertrophy 67–80% (8–12 reps), endurance below 67%. The calculator works out the actual weights for each percentage for you.
Is it accurate?
It's an estimate, not a guarantee — your real 1RM depends on technique, the day and the exercise. Still, it's a very useful guide for planning. Never test a true 1RM without a warm-up and a spotter.
How often should I recalculate my 1RM?
Roughly every 4 to 6 weeks, or whenever you hit a new PR in an exercise. Strength changes faster than you think, and an outdated 1RM leads to sets that are too light or too heavy.
Does the calculator work for machines and isolation exercises?
Technically yes, but the formulas were developed on basic compound lifts (squat, deadlift, bench press). For machines and isolation work the estimate is less reliable, because machine setup and range of motion affect the result.
