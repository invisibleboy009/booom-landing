Training split: full body, upper/lower, PPL or bro split

Updated September 20, 2026 · 7 min read

There is no best split. There is the split you can do consistently, that fits your week and gives every muscle group enough work. Here are the four most common options with sample weeks and a simple way to choose.

What a training split is

A split is a way to divide muscle groups and exercises across training days. There is no magic scheme. Results are decided mainly by weekly volume (how many hard sets per muscle), progression of the load and consistency. The split is only a tool that spreads those three things across your week.

So when choosing, don’t ask what an influencer does. Ask how many days you can realistically make, how you feel after training, and whether the plan is enjoyable enough that you will keep it up for at least six months.

If you have an injury, pain or a chronic condition, talk to a doctor or physiotherapist about your plan. This article is a general overview, not a personal training programme.

Full body: 2 to 3 days

You train the whole body in every session, typically a squat or lunge, a press, a pull and something for the trunk. Each muscle gets hit 2 to 3 times a week and you practise the main lifts often.

  • Fits: beginners, people with 2 to 3 free days, and anyone who also runs or plays another sport.
  • Downside: sessions run longer, and for advanced lifters it is hard to fit enough volume into an hour.
DayExercises
Monday (A)Squat, bench press, barbell row, plank
Wednesday (B)Romanian deadlift, overhead press, pull-up or lat pulldown, lunges
Friday (A)Same as Monday, with a different exercise variation or rep range

Next week, start with session B so that A and B alternate.

To begin with, 5 to 6 exercises per session of 2 to 3 sets each are enough. Add load rather than more exercises. A session like this usually takes around 45 to 60 minutes.

Upper/lower: 4 days

Two days you train the upper body, two days the lower body. Each muscle is trained twice a week and sessions are shorter than in full body. For most people with 3 to 4 days a week, it is a good middle ground.

DayFocusExercises
MondayUpperBench press, row, overhead press, pull-up, biceps, triceps
TuesdayLowerSquat, Romanian deadlift, lunges, calves, trunk
ThursdayUpperIncline press, cable row, lateral raise, biceps, triceps
FridayLowerDeadlift or hip thrust, front squat, leg curl, calves

Push / pull / legs: 3 to 6 days

Push is pressing (chest, shoulders, triceps), pull is pulling (back, biceps) and legs are legs. On 3 days you train each group once a week, on 6 days twice. With 4 to 5 days the cycle simply rolls over the weeks.

DayFocus
MondayPush: bench press, overhead press, triceps
TuesdayPull: row, pull-up, biceps
WednesdayLegs: squat, Romanian deadlift, calves
ThursdayPush (different exercise variations)
FridayPull (different exercise variations)
SaturdayLegs (different exercise variations)
SundayRest

Six days is a lot. You can handle it if you have the time and good sleep; otherwise it is worth starting PPL on 3 to 4 days.

Bro split: honest pros and cons

One or two muscle groups a day: Monday chest, Tuesday back, Wednesday legs, Thursday shoulders, Friday arms.

  • Pros: lots of volume per muscle in one session, a simple head space, good pumps, easy to plan and fun.
  • Cons: you train each muscle roughly once a week. Research commonly suggests that at the same weekly volume about twice a week tends to be more effective. A missed session means you don’t train that muscle for 14 days.

A bro split can make sense for advanced lifters with plenty of time and for people who love sticking to it. For a beginner, though, it usually offers nothing that full body or upper/lower wouldn’t do better.

A compromise: if you love a bro layout, adjust it so that the bigger muscle groups (chest, back, legs) come up twice a week and the smaller ones (shoulders, arms) are done as add-ons on those same days.

How to choose a split

Start from the number of days you can keep up for months, not from the ideal plan on paper.

Your situationSensible choice
2 to 3 days a weekFull body
4 days a weekUpper/lower
5 to 6 days a weekPPL (twice a week) or upper/lower plus an extra day
BeginnerFull body, then upper/lower
Advanced, more timePPL, upper/lower or bro split by preference
Goal: strengthFull body or upper/lower, main lifts often
Goal: hypertrophyUpper/lower or PPL, each muscle roughly twice a week
Weaker recovery, stress, little sleepFewer days and fewer sets, leaning towards full body
Irregular scheduleFull body, because a missed day breaks nothing

If you are torn between two options, pick the one with fewer days that you can keep up even in a hectic week. You can change your split after a few months, but consistency is the foundation.

How many sets per muscle per week

As a rough guide, about 10 to 20 hard sets per muscle group per week are commonly cited for muscle growth. A hard set is a set close to failure, usually with 1 to 3 reps in reserve. Individual differences are large.

  • Start lower, around 10 sets per muscle per week, and add only when progress stalls and recovery allows it.
  • In upper/lower that is roughly 5 sets per muscle per session, in full body 3 to 4 sets per session.
  • A set that works several muscles (say, bench press for chest and triceps) mainly counts towards the main target of the exercise.

More is not always better. If your performance drops or everything hurts, volume is probably higher than you can handle.

Example: in upper/lower, chest gets 2 sessions of 5 sets each (bench press, incline press), which is 10 sets a week. If progress stalls after a few weeks and recovery is good, add 1 to 2 sets per session, so 12 to 14 a week.

Combining lifting with running, Hyrox and CrossFit

If you run or prepare for Hyrox alongside lifting, your split must not pile up fatigue. A few rules:

  • Pick your two hardest days and spread them out. Don’t schedule heavy legs the day before a key run or intervals.
  • Full body 2 to 3 times a week or upper/lower 4 times combines most easily with running.
  • Where possible, separate a heavy lower-body session and a quality run by about 24 to 48 hours.
  • If a race is your main goal, treat lifting as a supplement and watch how you feel on your runs.

Log it, or you won’t know if it works

You can judge a split only after weeks of data. Log weights, reps and sets as described in Training log, and add load according to progressive overload. You can track your strength roughly in the 1RM calculator.

FAQ

Which training split is best for a beginner?

Usually full body 2 to 3 times a week. You train each muscle often, you repeat the technique of the main lifts, and a simple plan is easy to stick to. Upper/lower over 4 days is a sensible next step.

How many days a week is enough?

For a beginner, usually 2 to 3 days. With regular training and gradual load increases, that is enough for solid progress. More days means more room for volume, not automatically better results.

Is a bro split bad?

It is not bad, just often less efficient. You train each muscle roughly once a week, while research commonly suggests that at equal volume about twice a week tends to be somewhat better. If you enjoy it and stick to it, it still works.

How often should I train each muscle group?

Roughly twice a week is commonly recommended, because it spreads volume more easily and you practise the technique more often. It is not a hard rule; total weekly volume and consistency matter more.

Can I train every day?

You can, if some days are easy or you rotate different muscle groups. Still, one to two days off a week helps most people. More on recovery in Recovery and sleep.

When does it make sense to change my split?

When progress has stalled for several weeks despite enough sleep and food, or when your schedule changes. Don’t hop between plans every two weeks; you need at least 8 to 12 weeks to see whether one works.
