How Much Protein Do You Need per Day (and How to Spread It Out)

Updated September 20, 2026 · 7 min read

The number you know from the gym is “2 grams per kilo”. It is a reasonable estimate, but not always and not for everyone. Here is why, when less is enough and how to spread your protein across the day.

The short answer

For a healthy adult with a sedentary lifestyle, the reference intake is approximately 0.8 g of protein per kg of body weight per day. If you strength train, want to build muscle or are losing weight, most recommendations fall in the range of 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg.

Why this particular range

A 2018 meta-analysis (Morton et al., British Journal of Sports Medicine) pooled dozens of studies with strength training. Muscle gains improved with higher protein intake up to roughly 1.6 g per kg, and higher values mostly brought no further benefit. The upper bound of the confidence interval was around 2.2 g per kg, which is why this value is commonly given as a ceiling above which there is no point going.

More doesn’t always mean more muscle. Above roughly 2.2 g per kg it is usually just extra calories.

In a calorie deficit it pays to aim for the upper end: it helps preserve muscle mass and keeps you fuller. You can set your deficit in the calorie calculator.

Older adults and a calorie deficit

With age the body responds a little less strongly to protein, and without training and enough intake muscle mass slowly declines. Older adults are therefore commonly advised to eat more than the reference 0.8 g per kg. Some expert groups suggest roughly 1.0 to 1.2 g per kg for healthy older people, and more with regular strength training.

The same goes for a calorie deficit: when you eat less, the body also draws on muscle, so stay near the upper end. Keep the deficit moderate (roughly 15 to 20 % below your needs), more in Lose Fat Without Losing Muscle.

  • Smaller portions, more often. With a smaller appetite, five small meals beat three big ones.
  • Protein first. Eat it on your plate before you fill up on the side dish.
  • A strength stimulus. Protein alone won’t keep muscle. It needs load, see Progressive Overload.

This is general information, not medical advice. If you have kidney disease or another chronic condition, take medication or are pregnant, discuss your protein intake with a doctor or dietitian.

How to spread it across the day

A practical goal is 3 to 5 meals with about 0.3 to 0.4 g of protein per kg in each meal. For 80 kg that is roughly 25 to 35 g. The exact timing around your workout matters less than your total daily intake.

Plant-based protein sources

Plant sources usually have less protein per 100 g, and some are lower in certain amino acids (grains in lysine, legumes in methionine). The fix is simple: vary your sources through the day. No need to combine them in one meal.

On a fully plant-based diet, aim for the upper end of your range, because plant proteins are digested a little less completely. Soy, tempeh and seitan are the most concentrated sources. Seitan is wheat gluten, so skip it if you can’t tolerate gluten.

Hitting your target in practice

The gram number is only a starting point. Here is how to reach it without weighing every bite:

  • Estimate by hand. A palm-sized portion of meat or fish (cooked, about 100 to 150 g) gives roughly 25 to 35 g of protein. A handful of cottage cheese or yogurt is about 10 to 15 g.
  • Protein in every meal. Pick the protein first, then the side. It beats chasing 60 g at the end of the day.
  • Batch cooking. Once or twice a week, cook a big batch of chicken, legumes or tofu and portion it out.
  • Log for a few weeks. Until your estimates are reliable, record your food and watch the weekly average, not a single day.
Example: a target of 140 g across 4 meals is about 35 g per meal. A palm of chicken (~30 g) plus a yogurt covers it.

Work out your total calories in the calorie calculator and keep your progress in a training log. You can also log food and macros in the BOOOM app.

Protein powder and creatine

Protein powder is nothing magical, it is food in a convenient form. It makes sense when your appetite falls short, on the go, or when the last 20 to 30 g are missing from your plan. One scoop gives about 24 g (see the table above). It should fill the gap, not replace regular meals. Choose a product that states the protein content per serving.

Creatine is among the best-researched supplements for strength training. Healthy adults commonly take 3 to 5 g per day, and timing doesn’t matter. It is no miracle, just a small help with strength and performance. If you have kidney disease, take medication or are pregnant, check with a doctor first. This is not medical advice.

Myths and safety

  • “Protein ruins your kidneys.” In healthy people, studies have not confirmed this at commonly higher intakes. If you have kidney disease, talk to a doctor.
  • “You can’t build muscle without protein powder.” What matters is your total daily intake, not the form.
  • “The more, the better.” Once you go past the upper end of the range, you gain nothing more.

Log your intake and watch the weekly average. In your training log you can compare your intake with how your training is going, week by week.

FAQ

Do I have to drink a protein shake?

No. Protein powder is just a convenient source of protein. If you manage to get enough from food, you don’t need it.

Can the body only use 30 grams of protein at once?

No, that is a simplification. The body can use more, it just takes longer to digest. Spreading it over 3 to 5 meals is practical, not mandatory.

Is protein bad for the kidneys?

In healthy people, studies have not shown kidney damage at commonly higher intakes. If you have kidney disease, you need to discuss your protein intake with a doctor.

Is protein from a plant-based diet enough?

Yes. Combine different sources (legumes, tofu, grains, nuts) and aim for the upper end of the range.

Do I need to eat protein on rest days too?

Yes. Muscle repairs and builds between workouts, so keep your daily target about the same. The weekly average is what counts.

Which weight should I use to calculate protein if I am overweight?

People often use a realistic goal weight so the target doesn’t come out needlessly high. A dietitian or coach can give you a specific number.
