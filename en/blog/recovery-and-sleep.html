Recovery and Sleep: How to Recover Between Workouts

Updated September 20, 2026 · 7 min read

Training is only the stimulus. You get stronger and faster afterwards, when you eat, sleep and rest. If you sleep little and every session is all-out, progress slows and injury and burnout risk goes up. Here is how to handle it.

Why you improve during recovery

Training is a stress signal. It breaks down a little tissue, uses up energy stores and tires your nervous system. The adaptation, meaning stronger muscles, better endurance and tougher tendons, happens afterwards, in the hours and days when you eat, sleep and rest. More training without more recovery does not automatically mean more progress.

Two terms are worth knowing:

  • Overreaching is a short period of hard training in which performance dips for a few days to a couple of weeks, then bounces back and often improves after rest. Planned in small doses, it is normal.
  • Overtraining is a state where performance stays down for weeks or months despite rest, often with poor sleep, low mood and constant fatigue. It is much rarer than people say and usually comes from a long-term mismatch between load, food, sleep and life stress.

Hard work is fine as long as recovery keeps up. Work stress and a short night come out of the same budget as a heavy squat day.

Sleep: the base of recovery

Adults are commonly advised to sleep 7 to 9 hours a night. Needs are individual, so treat it as a range. If you wake up rested and feel alert during the day, you are probably close to your number.

Research suggests that short or poor sleep affects training in several ways:

  • Performance. Sessions feel harder, coordination and reaction time get worse, and you may lift less or run slower.
  • Appetite. After short nights people tend to feel hungrier and crave calorie-dense food, which makes a deficit harder to hold (more in how to lose fat and keep muscle).
  • Motivation. It is easier to skip a workout or cut the intensity, and mood is usually lower.

One bad night ruins nothing; the pattern over weeks counts. If 7 hours is not realistic, protecting it most nights beats one perfect night and chaos the rest.

Food and hydration for recovery

  • Protein. Roughly 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of body weight across the day supports muscle repair for people who lift. Details in how much protein per day.
  • Carbohydrates. After hard or long sessions, carbs refill muscle glycogen. This matters most when you train twice a day or have another demanding session soon, for example in Hyrox or running. A normal meal with rice, potatoes, pasta or bread plus protein is enough for most people.
  • Hydration. Drink regularly through the day; pale-yellow urine is a rough guide. After very sweaty sessions, get some salt from normal food too.

Eating too little is a common hidden reason for poor recovery. When cutting, keep the deficit moderate (roughly 15 to 20 % below maintenance; find yours in the calorie calculator). Creatine (3 to 5 g per day) is commonly used and considered safe for healthy adults, but it is optional.

Active recovery and rest days

Active recovery is light movement that adds no real fatigue: easy walking, gentle cycling or swimming, and mobility work. Keep the pace conversational. A relaxed 20 to 40 minute walk is underrated. Complete rest is fine too, and at least one such day per week is a good idea.

None of this replaces sleep, food and a sensible plan. Treat it as a bonus.

Warning signs and when to deload

  • Performance is falling or stuck for two weeks or more despite good effort, and the same weights feel heavier.
  • Resting heart rate is clearly higher than usual for several mornings in a row (a few beats either way is normal, so watch the trend).
  • Soreness or stiffness does not clear between sessions.
  • Mood, motivation and sleep are worse than usual: irritability, dreading workouts, waking at night.

If several signs show up together, a deload is a sensible response. Cut volume by roughly a third to a half (fewer sets), keep weights moderate and effort easy for about a week, then return to your plan. Many lifters plan a deload every 4 to 8 weeks anyway; more in progressive overload.

See a doctor if you have pain that does not improve or gets worse, dizziness, fatigue that lasts even after several weeks of rest, or an injury. We are not doctors, and this article does not replace an examination.

Track sleep and energy in your log

In your training log, note two more numbers next to your sets: how many hours you slept and how you feel on a scale of 1 to 5. It takes a few seconds.

  • Once a week, check whether weak sessions follow short nights.
  • Watch the trend in resting heart rate, not single mornings.
  • Note skipped deloads or long streaks of training days and compare them with performance.

After a few weeks you will see your own patterns, and decisions about rest get clearer. You can also do this in the BOOOM app.

FAQ

How much sleep do I need if I train hard?

Adults are commonly advised to get 7 to 9 hours a night. Needs are individual, so treat it as a range. If you wake up rested and stay alert during the day, you are probably close to your number. Regularly sleeping under 7 hours usually hurts strength and recovery.

Should I train if I slept badly?

One bad night is usually fine: shorten the session or take it a bit lighter. If you are chronically under-slept, ill, dizzy or feel faint, skip it and rest.

How many rest days per week do I need?

Most people do well with 1 to 2 days of light activity or complete rest per week. It depends on volume, intensity, age, sleep and food. If you feel tired for a long time, add rest before another workout.

Is napping useful?

A short nap of around 20 to 30 minutes in the first half of the day can help after a bad night. A long or late nap can make it harder to fall asleep in the evening.

Do I need supplements for recovery?

Start with the basics: sleep, enough food and protein, and a sensible plan. Protein powder is a convenient top-up, and creatine (3 to 5 g per day) is commonly used and considered safe for healthy adults. No supplement replaces sleep.

How do I know if I am overdoing it?

Typical signals are falling performance, a higher resting heart rate, persistent soreness and worse mood or sleep. Try a week-long deload. If nothing improves after several weeks, fatigue persists, or you feel pain or dizziness, see a doctor.
