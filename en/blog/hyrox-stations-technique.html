Hyrox Stations: Technique and Tactics for All 8

Updated September 20, 2026 · 7 min read

Hyrox is eight kilometres of running with eight stations in between, and the stations are where most races are won or lost. Good technique saves the energy you will need on kilometre six. Here is how to move through each station as efficiently as possible.

Why stations decide races

Anyone can run, but the stations reward technique. Two equally fit athletes can finish minutes apart because one wastes energy on sloppy technique. Mistakes also compound: every station is followed by a 1 km run on tired legs, so a badly executed station costs you twice.

That is why Hyrox is called "compromised running": your heart rate is high, your forearms are burning, and you still have to hold an even pace. Train them back to back, not only separately. The table shows what matters most at each station.

SkiErg and rowing: pull with legs and hips

Both are pulling machines with one idea: the legs and hips do the work, and the arms just connect you to the handle.

SkiErg. Reach up tall, hinge at the hips, bend your knees slightly and pull the handles down. Keep your arms fairly straight at first and finish the pull with your lats. Let the handles rise back in a relaxed way; that is where you breathe.

Rowing. On the drive the order is legs, hips, arms, reversed on the return. Most of the power comes from the leg push, and you finish with a slight lean back and the handle at the lower ribs.

Rhythm beats power. A higher damper setting is not automatically faster: it feels heavier and tires you sooner, so most people settle on a moderate one. Pace by feel and start slightly below what you think you can hold, because you still need your legs. If you can say a few words, you are about right.

Sled: push and pull

Push. Get low: hips below shoulders, back flat, arms extended or slightly bent, and push through the floor with short, powerful steps. A low body angle turns leg drive into forward motion, while an upright body pushes you down into the sled.

Keep your feet under your hips and push through the ball of the foot. Breathe out on each drive and do not hold your breath, the most common habit under heavy load.

Pull. Plant your feet, sit back and pull the rope hand over hand with your chest up. The power comes from leaning back and using your legs, not just your arms. Do not strangle the rope: a relaxed but firm grip saves your forearms for the farmers carry a few stations later.

Burpee broad jumps: rhythm, not explosiveness

This station is about rhythm. Step or jump back, chest to the floor, press up, bring your feet in and jump forward. Read the official rules for what counts as a valid rep and practise to that.

Jump length versus speed: a maximal jump costs a lot of energy, while a moderate jump you can repeat is faster overall. Pick a cadence you can hold and breathe as you stand up.

Pacing: people most often start too fast because the heart rate spikes. Find your rhythm from the first rep, and if you need a break, take it at a planned point rather than when you fall apart.

Farmers carry and sandbag lunges

Farmers carry. Grip is the limiter. Stand tall with your shoulders back and take short, quick steps. Breathe steadily. If grip is going, put the weights down briefly and shake out your hands instead of dropping them.

Sandbag lunges. Keep the bag high and tight on your shoulders with an upright torso. Your front knee should track in line with your toes, and you touch the back knee down lightly instead of dropping onto it. Make each step deep enough to count but no longer than necessary. Breathe once per step.

Wall balls: depth, throw path and set plan

This is the last station. Keep your squat depth the same every time and to the standard the rules require. Throw with your legs: drive up from the bottom of the squat and let the momentum carry the ball to the target. Catch it close to your chest.

Breathing: inhale at the top or on the way down and exhale on the throw.

Break strategy: plan your sets in advance. Decide before the station how many sets you will split the total reps into, and take a short pause of a few breaths between them. A planned pause costs less than a forced one when your strength gives out mid-set.

The roxzone: transitions and mental reset

The roxzone is the area between the run and the station, and time spent in it counts.

Walk or jog? Walking a few steps to bring your heart rate down can be a sensible tactic, especially before technical stations like wall balls, but long walks add up. Decide in advance when and for how long.

Mental reset: one deep breath, one cue word for the next station (for example "low" or "legs"), and let the previous one go. Look at the next station, not at your watch.

The most common station mistakes

  • Starting too fast. The early stations look easy, so people overcook them.
  • Pulling with the arms only. SkiErg and rowing are about legs and hips.
  • Standing upright on the sled push. You push into the floor instead of forward.
  • Gripping too hard. The forearms give out early.
  • Wall balls without a plan. Breaks arrive when you do not want them.
  • Ignoring the rules. A rep that does not count costs time, so read the standards for your category.
  • Never training tired. You have never run straight after a station.

FAQ

Which Hyrox station is the hardest?

It depends on your strengths and weaknesses. For some it is the sled push, for others wall balls or the farmers carry. You find out by measuring your splits in a simulation and comparing them with your running.

How do I stop my grip failing on the farmers carry?

Hold the handles firmly but do not squeeze harder than needed, take short quick steps and breathe steadily. If your grip is about to go, put the weights down briefly and shake out your hands rather than dropping them.

Should I break wall balls into sets?

Yes, with a plan made in advance: decide how many sets you will split the total into, and take a short pause of a few breaths between them. A planned pause costs less than a forced long stop when your strength runs out.

Do I need a real sled to prepare?

It is not essential. The low body position, leg drive and rope pulling can be practised with backpack lunges or band rows. Do try a real sled with race-style load at least once before the event.

Should I walk or jog through the roxzone?

A few steps of walking to bring your heart rate down can be a sensible tactic, especially before technical stations. Long walks add up, so decide in advance when and for how long, and test it in training.

How often should I practise the stations?

Roughly once or twice a week per station, with more hybrid sessions where you alternate stations with running. For a week-by-week layout, see the 8-week Hyrox plan.
